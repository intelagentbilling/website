In short

A special visit premium is extra payment for making a special trip to render an insured service — being called in rather than being there anyway. It always rides alongside a base service, never alone. The amount depends on three things: the day (weekday, weekend, statutory holiday), the time band (daytime, evening, night), and the setting (hospital in-patient, emergency department, office, home). Most premiums pay a travel component for the trip plus a smaller per-patient component for each person seen on it.

Special visit premiums exist because the Schedule treats a 2 a.m. call-in and a Tuesday afternoon ward round as different kinds of work, even when the clinical service is identical. The base fee pays for the service. The premium pays for the disruption.

They are also the part of the Schedule most likely to be left on the table. Not because physicians do not know they exist, but because claiming one correctly requires recording three facts at the time — when you were called, when you arrived, and how many patients you saw on that trip — and those facts are gone by the time anyone sits down to bill.

The two components

Most special visit situations pay in two parts:

  • A travel premium — paid once per trip, for the first patient seen. This is the larger component, and it is what the premium is really for.
  • A per-patient premium — a smaller amount for each additional patient seen on the same trip.

The practical consequence is that a single call-in to see four patients does not pay four travel premiums. It pays one travel premium and three additional-patient premiums. Billing four travel premiums is a reliable way to get three of them reversed on a later Remittance Advice.

The trip is the unit, not the patient. Get this one right and most of the rest follows. The question the Schedule is asking is "how many special trips did you make?", not "how many patients did you see?"

Day and time bands

The Schedule divides the week into bands, and the premium rate follows the band. The structure, in broad terms:

  • Weekday daytime — the baseline. Special visit premiums here are the smallest, and in many settings do not apply at all.
  • Weekday evenings — a defined evening window on Monday to Friday.
  • Nights — the overnight window, which attracts the highest premiums.
  • Saturdays, Sundays and statutory holidays — daytime hours on these days are treated as premium time in their own right.

The exact boundaries of each window, and the exact amounts, are set in the General Preamble to the Schedule of Benefits and change with each fee agreement. Do not carry a remembered cut-off time from a previous year into this one.

Friday evening is not the weekend. Several premiums group Friday evening with Saturday and Sunday, and several do not. Billing a weekend premium for a Friday evening attracts rejection code V4 — "date of service was not a Saturday, Sunday, or a statutory holiday" — which is one of the more common premium rejections.

Setting matters

The same call-in pays differently depending on where you went. The Schedule distinguishes, among others:

  • Hospital in-patient services
  • Emergency department services
  • Office or clinic visits outside normal hours
  • Home visits
  • Long-term care and other institutional settings

The service location indicator on the claim has to agree with the premium claimed. A mismatch produces rejection code V68 — the service location indicator is wrong for the service.

Groups and hospital departments

Premiums get more complicated when the claim is submitted under a ministry group number rather than by a solo physician. Some premiums fold their companion codes differently for group submissions, and a premium billed as though it were solo work can be reduced or refused when the same service under a group would have been paid.

If you bill through an emergency group, a hospitalist service or an academic department, the group's billing conventions are part of the rules, not an administrative detail. See departments and groups for how group billing works in practice.

Capturing the facts at the time

Every premium problem traces back to information that was available at 3 a.m. and gone by the following Tuesday. Three facts, recorded when they happen, solve almost all of it:

  1. The time you were called and the time you arrived

    Not the time you wrote the note. The band is determined by the visit, and a note written at 7 a.m. about a 2 a.m. call-in will quietly be billed as a morning visit.

  2. Which patient was first on the trip

    The travel premium attaches to one of them. If the order is not recorded, it gets guessed, and a guess that puts the travel premium on the wrong encounter is hard to unpick later.

  3. Whether you were already on site

    The distinction between being called in and being there anyway is the whole basis of the premium. It is also the distinction nobody writes down.

Frequently asked questions

What is an OHIP special visit premium?

An additional amount payable when a physician makes a special trip to render an insured service outside the normal course of a scheduled practice — for example being called in at night, on a weekend, or to a hospital from elsewhere. It is billed alongside the service itself, never on its own, and the amount depends on the day, the time band, and the setting.

Can I bill a special visit premium if I was already at the hospital?

Generally no. The premium pays for the special trip, so if you were already on site for other work, the trip did not happen. The General Preamble to the Schedule of Benefits sets out exactly what counts, and the distinction between being called in and already being present is the one most often got wrong.

Why was my special visit premium rejected with code V4?

V4 means the date of service was not a Saturday, Sunday or statutory holiday. A weekend or holiday premium was billed for a weekday. The fix is to rebill with the premium that matches the actual day and time band of the visit.

What does rejection code DF mean on a premium?

DF means the corresponding procedure was invalid, omitted, or paid at zero. A premium is an add-on and has to travel with the base service it attaches to. If the base service was not billed, was rejected, or was paid at nothing, the premium has nothing to attach to and falls away with it.
