A group number tells the ministry to pay a set of physicians together. Each claim still names the physician who did the work, but the payment lands in the group's account and arrives on one Remittance Advice covering everybody. What a group number actually is

Two identifiers travel on every claim:

  • The billing number — the individual physician who rendered the service. This never changes and is always present.
  • The group number — present only when the claim is being billed under a group arrangement. It tells the ministry which account to pay.

The group number does not replace the billing number; it sits alongside it. The ministry still knows exactly who did the work, which matters for anything scoped to a physician — per-patient limits, daily caps, and the premiums whose eligibility depends on what else that physician billed that day.

Where the money goes

To the group's account, in one deposit, with one Remittance Advice listing every claim from every physician in the group.

That is convenient for the ministry and awkward for everyone else, because the thing a physician in a group wants to know — what did my work earn this month — is not what the RA is organised around. Somebody has to break it out by rendering physician, and the breakdown has to survive rejections, adjustments and reversals that arrive on later RAs than the original claims.

In a small group this is usually done in a spreadsheet, and it works until it does not: the usual failure is a reversal landing two months after the claim, by which point the spreadsheet that allocated the original payment has already been closed and distributed. A spreadsheet records a payment on the day it arrives; it has no way of knowing the payment changed afterwards.

This is the problem IntelAGENT was built around. Each physician's share is read from the settled position of their own claims, not from a snapshot taken on payment day, so an adjustment or reversal arriving two RAs later changes that physician's number instead of landing in a reconciliation pile. Premiums behave differently in a group

This is the part that surprises people, and it is a genuine rule rather than a quirk of any particular software.

Several premiums exist as a base code plus a companion code. Billed by a solo physician, both lines are payable and both appear. Billed under some group arrangements, the companion is folded into the group's payment rather than paid separately — the work is recognised, but the claim does not show two paid lines the way the solo version does.

What this means practically:

  1. A group claim that looks underpaid may be correct

    If you compare a group claim against a solo claim for the same service, the line counts can legitimately differ. Chasing it as a rejection wastes time.

  2. Your expected-payment figure has to know about the group

    Any estimate that prices a group claim as though it were solo will over-predict, and every month will look like a shortfall. The estimate has to be group-aware or the reconciliation is permanently noisy.

  3. The specific codes are in the Schedule

    Which premiums fold and under what arrangement is set out in the Schedule of Benefits. It is specific enough that it is worth reading for your own group rather than taking from a summary.

Failures that only happen in groups

Ordered by how often they turn up:

  • Group number missing on some claims. Usually a new physician whose profile was set up without it, or a locum covering in the group. The claims pay to the wrong account — the individual rather than the group — which is not a rejection and so is easy to miss until someone reconciles.
  • Group number on claims that should be solo. The reverse, and the one that annoys physicians most, because their own clinic work has been paid into the group.
  • A new physician billing before being added to the group. The ministry has to know the physician is in the group; claims submitted under a group number for a physician not on record are refused.
  • Departures. A physician who has left still has claims in flight. Removing them from the group too early strands those claims.
  • One wrong default, dozens of identical rejections. Group and facility defaults are set once and applied to everything, so a single wrong value produces a whole family of rejections that are all fixed the same way. Work them as a batch rather than in arrival order — see claim rejections.

Groups and hospital work

Most hospital-based groups — emergency departments, hospitalist services, anaesthesia departments, on-call rotas — bill under a group number for the hospital work and not for anything else. A physician in that position has two streams, two Remittance Advices, and two sets of expectations about what should be paid.

The practical requirement is that your billing setup can hold both without you having to remember which one you are in. Defaults that follow the facility or the shift rather than being set globally per physician are what makes this reliable; defaults set once per physician are what causes the missing-group-number failure above.

Frequently asked questions

What is an OHIP group number?

It is a ministry-issued number identifying a group of physicians who are paid together. Claims submitted under the group number are paid to the group's account rather than to the individual physician, even though each claim still carries the rendering physician's billing number.

Does group billing change the fee code I use?

The fee codes are the same, but some premiums behave differently in a group — a companion code that is payable to a solo physician may be folded into the group's payment instead. Which premiums those are is set out in the Schedule of Benefits.

Can one physician bill both solo and in a group?

Yes, and many do — hospital work under a group number and clinic work solo, for example. Each claim carries the group number or does not, so the two streams stay separate and appear on different Remittance Advices.

Who sees a group's Remittance Advice?

The RA goes to the account the claims were submitted under, so a group RA covers every physician in the group. Splitting it back out per physician is the group's own work, not something the ministry does.
